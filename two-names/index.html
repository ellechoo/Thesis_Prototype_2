How Korean American families came to have two names, and how each person carries both today

This archive collects the stories of immigrants who chose an English name after arriving in the US, and their US-born children, who were usually given both a Korean and an English name. Each person's names are drawn as two lines that start from the same point and split, run side by side, or fade.

Finding out what your name means

Each Korean syllable can be written with many different Hanja, and each one has its own meaning. Only the people who named you know which ones they chose, so this site won't guess for you. Here's how you can find out:

  • Ask your family. You could send a parent or grandparent this message:

  • Check family documents. If you were registered in Korea, your family relations certificate (가족관계증명서) shows your name in Hanja. Baby books, first birthday (돌) records, or a naming certificate from a name maker (작명소) may also have it.

If you don't know yet, leave everything below as "Not sure." Your meaning will be marked as unknown, and you can add a new entry once you find out.

Explore what each syllable could mean

If you're curious, here are the possibilities. Common characters (the 1,800 taught in Korean schools) are listed first. If one sounds familiar, you can select it. It will be saved as a guess, not a confirmed meaning.

The characters are every one on Korea's official list of Hanja allowed in names (인명용 한자). English definitions are from the Unicode Han Database (Unihan) and are mostly based on Chinese usage. When you select a character, its Korean meaning (음훈) is looked up on Wiktionary when available.

About

Two Names is a thesis prototype exploring how names are given, chosen, and carried across generations of Korean American families. It starts with Korean and English names, with the goal of expanding to other languages and communities later.

How to read the lines

  • Starting point: both names start from the same point, the person.
  • Where a line starts: a name given at birth starts at the beginning; a name taken on later branches off at the year it began.
  • Color: who gave the name — parents, grandparents, self, teacher, someone else.
  • Thickness: how often the name is used today.
  • Filled vs. outlined: a filled line is a legal name; an outlined line is unofficial.
  • Solid, dashed, dotted: the name's meaning is known by the person, taken from a source or guessed, or unknown.

Privacy

The archive only collects first names. You can show them or stay anonymous. To remove an entry, contact the project.
